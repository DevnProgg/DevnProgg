Beyond the Known Pattern: What a Synthetic Transaction Graph Taught Us About Label Scarcity and Typology Shift

Article

A research-paper-style blog post based on the LaundroGraph research project available at source code. All numbers are taken from saved artifacts; all data are synthetic, so nothing here is a claim about real-world AML performance.

Abstract

Financial-crime detection has two persistent problems: labels are scarce, and the typologies we trained against keep changing. LaundroGraph is a controlled, synthetic transaction-graph benchmark built to study exactly those two problems. We froze a time-ordered split (signature 7cdbe8e10a4be50f), fixed an alert budget, and compared logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, Isolation Forest, and a two-layer GraphSAGE on the same test set under (A) shrinking training-label fractions and (B) leaving one crime typology entirely out of training and validation.

The results are not the "graph wins" story that the motivation might suggest. Under label scarcity, tree baselines remained marginally ahead on PR-AUC, with the GNN competitive only at the 5% label fraction. Under held-out typology shift, supervised tabular models clearly beat GraphSAGE on precision/recall at a 100-alert budget. Ablations showed that a generator shortcut (the channel field alone reaches 0.9076 ROC-AUC) explains part of the strong tabular performance, and that content features : not graph-feature blocks : cleared random-block controls in the GNN necessity test. The value of the project is therefore not a model leaderboard; it is a traceable protocol that separates scarcity from shift, quantifies its own confounds, and refuses to turn synthetic results into operational claims.

1. Why build another benchmark?

A single suspicious transfer is often unremarkable. What makes layering, circular flows, mule chains, and smurfing detectable is the relationship among many ordinary-looking transactions. Transaction-level models cannot represent that relationship; graph models can. That is the intuition.

But testing the intuition needs ground truth that real banks rarely have: investigation-verified labels, known typologies, and the counterfactual "same data without topology." A synthetic generator gives all three at the cost of realism. LaundroGraph injects crime events into a functioning synthetic economy with ten legitimate account archetypes, records exactly which transactions participated in which event, and preserves the raw data and generation config (configs/generator.yaml, data/raw/lg_c12e9cbd5b64/).

The research questions were defined before the runs:

  • RQ1: Does graph-informed modelling beat transaction-level baselines on coordinated typologies?
  • RQ2: Does topology add value beyond transaction attributes under the same model class?
  • RQ3: Are any gains concentrated on multi-account schemes rather than isolated anomalies?
  • RQ4: Do typologies have distinguishable local graph signatures?
  • RQ5/RQ6 (secondary): GAT vs GraphSAGE, and evidence-grounded investigator summaries.

The design also stated falsification criteria up front: if the graph advantage disappeared under paired comparison, if R3 ≈ R2, or if the interaction contrast was ~0, the report would say so. It did, and it does.

Two protocol details matter more than they look:

  1. Unlabelled ≠ negative. In label-scarcity runs, unlabelled training transactions stay in the graph but are masked out of the loss. The test set never shrinks.
  2. Held-out ≠ future. In typology-shift runs, the held-out typology is removed from training and validation and threshold selection. But one static generator rule set produced all typologies, and every event sits in the same 9,848-node giant component. So this is "unseen by the model," not "unseen by the data-generating process," and component-disjoint evaluation was infeasible.

Models: logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, Isolation Forest on transaction attributes (R1); XGBoost and friends with graph-derived node features (R4); and GraphSAGE : two SAGEConv layers, hidden size 64, mean aggregation, BatchNorm/ReLU/dropout 0.2, an edge MLP over [h_src || h_dst || edge_features], train-only pos_weight, early stopping (patience 15), validation-selected threshold. GAT (RQ5) was not run; it remains Tier 2 future work.

3. Finding 1 : Under label scarcity, the GNN is competitive but does not win

Experiment A varies the fraction of available training labels while holding the test set fixed.

Model frac = 1.0 frac = 0.25 frac = 0.10 frac = 0.05
GraphSAGE 0.932 ± 0.004 0.925 ± 0.008 0.916 ± 0.007 0.900 ± 0.010
XGBoost (R1) 0.958 ± 0.021 0.951 ± 0.022 0.941 ± 0.028 0.917 ± 0.043
Random forest (R1) 0.957 ± 0.023 0.946 ± 0.026 0.928 ± 0.027 0.902 ± 0.037
Logistic regression (R1) 0.898 ± 0.031 0.891 ± 0.032 0.877 ± 0.035 0.868 ± 0.027
Isolation Forest 0.414 ± 0.122 0.407 ± 0.113 0.388 ± 0.108 0.362 ± 0.097

Two honest readings:

  • Degrading labels hurts everyone, but the supervised tabular models degrade gently and still lead. At full labels, XGBoost's 0.958 mean PR-AUC exceeds GraphSAGE's 0.932.
  • At 5% labels the gap narrows substantially (0.917 vs 0.900) and XGBoost's variance rises sharply (±0.043). This is where a graph model becomes competitive on this benchmark : not where it dominates.

The interpretation offered in the evaluation report is deliberately cautious: no winner is asserted, because the R4 ablation and GNN grid must remain directly comparable. This is a measured ordering under one generator family, not a law about graph learning.

4. Finding 2 : Fixed alert budgets flatten the differences

Investigators do not consume PR-AUC; they consume a queue of alerts. At a budget of 100 transactions on a test set with 401 positives, every supervised model achieves roughly 0.25 recall and near-1.00 precision.

Model P@100 / R@100 (full labels) P@250 / R@250 (full labels) P@250 / R@250 (5% labels)
GraphSAGE 0.996 / 0.248 0.962 / 0.600 0.926 / 0.577
XGBoost (R1) 1.000 / 0.249 1.000 / 0.623 0.998 / 0.623
Random forest (R1) 1.000 / 0.249 0.996 / 0.622 0.994 / 0.620
Logistic regression (R1) 1.000 / 0.249 0.984 / 0.613 0.954 / 0.596
Isolation Forest 0.462 / 0.115 0.592 / 0.369 0.434 / 0.270

This is an important calibration for any graph-learning claim: at the top of the ranking, a strong tree model already fills a small alert queue almost perfectly. The interesting differences appear in the middle of the ranking and in error composition, not in the first 100 alerts.

5. Finding 3 : Typology shift is where the graph model clearly underperforms

Experiment B holds out one coordinated typology at a time. Precision/recall at K=100, restricted to held-out-typology positives:

Held-out typology Held-out test positives GraphSAGE P/R XGBoost (R1) P/R Random forest P/R Logistic regression P/R
circular_flow 60 0.26 / 0.43 0.60 / 1.00 0.58 / 0.97 0.52 / 0.87
layering 46 0.13 / 0.28 0.42 / 0.91 0.36 / 0.78 0.34 / 0.74
mule_network 156 0.26 / 0.17 0.67 / 0.43 0.59 / 0.38 0.43 / 0.28
smurfing_network 182 0.36 / 0.20 0.95 / 0.52 0.60 / 0.33 1.00 / 0.55

GraphSAGE trails the supervised tabular baselines on every held-out typology, especially on mule and smurfing networks. The pattern is consistent with the transductive setup: the GNN learned from a full-window graph whose structure and neighbours were shared across train and test, so it had less reason to learn a transferable rule for a mechanism it never saw.

The correct conclusion is narrow and useful: under this static-rule generator, this evaluator, and this budget, learned neighbourhood aggregation did not generalize to held-out typologies as well as tree ensembles on transaction attributes. It is not evidence that GNNs cannot generalize; it is evidence that our benchmark did not demonstrate it.

6. Finding 4 : The generator left fingerprints, and we measured them

A benchmark is only as honest as its confounds. Here, the channel field alone achieves 0.9076 ROC-AUC on training data (experiments/baselines/ablation_channel.json). Removing channel from the no-graph XGBoost features drops PR-AUC from 0.9371 to 0.8892.

That matters because injected crime was assigned distinctive channel/type combinations. The tabular baselines are exploiting a signal that is real in this generator but may not exist in a bank. Any reported graph advantage : or lack of one : has to be read against this shortcut. It is also the most likely reason XGBoost's alert-queue performance is so strong.

False positives follow the same fingerprint. For the full-label XGBoost R1 run (TP=343, FP=44, FN=58, TN=18,907; 2.32 FP per 1,000 legitimate transactions), false positives concentrate in wire/transfer (20.3% within-cell FP rate) and internal/transfer (22.9%) : legitimate transactions that happen to share the injected envelope.

7. Finding 5 : Errors are concentrated, not uniform

Missed positives are strongly typology-dependent (XGBoost R1, full labels, pooled seeds):

Typology Positive transactions False-negative rate
smurfing_network 455 0.374
mule_network 390 0.269
isolated_anomaly 50 0.100
layering 115 0.087
circular_flow 150 0.000
structuring 845 0.012

At 5% labels, smurfing FN rises to 0.418 and mule to 0.326. Under holdout, the held-out typology is not necessarily the worst case : other complex typologies remain hard too. This is a measured error distribution in a synthetic ecosystem, not a claim that smurfing is intrinsically harder in the real world.

8. Finding 6 : Explanations are useful, unstable in places, and do not certify the graph

Three explainability results are worth carrying into future work:

  1. Ranking stability is model-dependent. Across seeds, Spearman correlation of feature rankings is 0.729 for XGBoost tree importance at full labels (0.884 at 5%), versus 1.000 for logistic-regression coefficients at full labels. XGBoost's individual feature ranks are provisional; its aggregate error curve is not.
  2. Held-out profiles look similar in feature space. SHAP rank agreement between held-out and known positives ranges 0.915–0.988 across the four typologies. That shows the same input families drive the model's dependence; it does not prove the same behavioural mechanism.
  3. Graph feature blocks did not clear random controls. In the GraphSAGE edge-feature permutation test (Experiment A, full labels, seed 1), excess drop over a same-width random block was +0.083 for amount, +0.019 for channel, +0.019 for tx_type, −0.027 for graph_degree_flow, +0.001 for graph_network, and −0.077 for merchant. Content blocks matter; the tested graph blocks provide no positive evidence of necessity beyond an arbitrary block of the same size. Edge-feature permutation also does not prove that message passing itself helps : that needs retrained structure ablations.

The research audit's verdict on the claim "graph message passing helps" was explicit: not supported by reported ablations. We kept it that way.

9. What the research questions actually got

  • RQ1 / H1 : graph models beat transaction baselines on coordinated typologies: not supported in this grid. Tree baselines lead on overall PR-AUC and on held-out typologies; GraphSAGE is competitive only at the lowest label fraction.
  • RQ2 / H2 : topology adds value beyond attributes: not established. The channel shortcut is strong, and the R4 ladder must be read together with it; graph blocks did not exceed random controls in the GNN probe.
  • RQ3 / H3 : gains concentrate on multi-account schemes: no positive interaction demonstrated. Errors do concentrate on complex typologies (smurfing, mule), but that is an error-distribution result, not a graph-gain result.
  • RQ4 / H4 : typologies have distinct graph signatures: retained as an analysis hypothesis; the honesty constraints in the audit keep any such description descriptive rather than predictive.
  • RQ5 / GAT: not evaluated (Tier 2).
  • RQ6 / evidence-grounded investigator assistant: demonstrated as a prototype with deterministic summaries, grounding validation, and audit logs; it is not part of the detection-performance claim.

10. Threats to validity : stated prominently, not buried

  • Synthetic only. Amounts are LSL-denominated design units, not calibrated to any real banking population.
  • Static generator rules. "Held-out" means unseen by the model, not unseen by the generating process.
  • Transductive graph. One full-window graph; all events in one giant component; train and test share neighbourhoods. No out-of-distribution structural generalization claim.
  • Generator shortcut. Channel/type are correlated with injection and dominate several results.
  • Correlated features. Permutation and SHAP split or duplicate credit among correlated inputs; ranks are not additive causal budgets.
  • Threshold and budget dependence. FP/FN counts depend on the validation-selected threshold; the alert-budget table is the operational complement.
  • Partial GNN faithfulness. Local probes exist; no full subgraph explainer and no retrained structure ablation.
  • One generator family. Multiple seeds are not multiple worlds.

An independent audit scored the work "conditionally ready": careful protocols, good reproducibility, strong explainability guardrails, with transductive generalization and shortcut quantification as the major open risks.

11. Takeaways for anyone building AML research tooling

  1. Run the protocol before the architecture. Frozen splits, masked losses, validation-only thresholds, and saved predictions make a null result readable. Without them, the same numbers become an unfalsifiable story.
  2. Measure your shortcut. A single field giving 0.9076 ROC-AUC is a finding, not a nuisance to hide.
  3. Report the alert queue, not just the curve. Precision@100 near 1.00 for every supervised model changes what "improvement" means operationally.
  4. Treat graph structure as a hypothesis. This benchmark did not confirm that message passing adds beyond content; the ablations say so, and that is a legitimate scientific outcome.
  5. Keep synthetic results synthetic. The contribution is a reproducible lab and an honest measurement discipline, not deployment readiness.
